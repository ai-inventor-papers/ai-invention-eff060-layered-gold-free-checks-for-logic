The question

Can you tell a wrong logic translation from a right one without a gold formula?

A sentence can be formalised correctly in many ways, and gold formulas are scarce and often wrong. The paper's answer: ask several independent LLM families to translate the same sentence, and score the candidate by how many of their translations a solver finds not equivalent to it.

This is . On the test ( candidates from long templated sentences under a ) it separates erroneous from correct candidates far better than a cheap LLM judge.

      Where it fails

      Three ways consensus goes wrong

      Does consensus punish a correct translation that most peers did not write?

      Some templates admit two accepted readings: a weaker one and a stronger one (for example, "unless" read as a one-way or a two-way condition). Both are labelled correct. Most peers write the weak one, so a correct strong-reading translation is outvoted. The bars count correct candidates flagged at score 0.5 or above, computed from the per-candidate records.

      What the paper does not show

      • Free vocabulary is not confirmed. The only result confirmed on an independent test set is the shared-vocabulary long-sentence test. The Dataset E result met its pre-registered criterion but Dataset E was also used for development. A fresh 550-sentence set, a second long-sentence sample and RCOMP under free vocabulary were all budget-stopped before reaching testability.
      • Renames break it. Two formulas with different predicate names are never Z3-equivalent under an identity map. On the perturbation suite the nonce-rename false-alarm rate is 0.76.
      • Shared mistakes are invisible. On Dataset E, 38% of errors are endorsed by the majority: 57% of structural errors but 5% of polarity errors. Text checks catch some of these, yet fusion does not significantly beat consensus alone (stratified +0.011, not significant).
      • Every bridge trades one error for another. Name-free alignment, hybrid matching and gloss-gated checking each lower divergence and raise endorsement. The gloss-gated checker failed its development gate (0.696 vs 0.742). Giving peers the candidate's own symbols lowered d (0.139 vs 0.323) but raised e (0.459 vs 0.173), for AUROC 0.614 against 0.770.
      • Templated text. The confirmed test uses templated sentences with a shared symbol list, which is narrower than real NL-to-FOL, where generators choose their own names.
